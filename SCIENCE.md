## 1. What Aaina measures, and with what

| Domain | Instrument | Source | License basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relationship satisfaction | Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI-16) | Funk & Rogge (2007), *Journal of Family Psychology*, 21(4), 572–583 | Distributed free by the authors for research/clinical use with citation |
| Attachment (anxiety, avoidance) | ECR-Short Form (12 items) | Wei, Russell, Mallinckrodt & Vogel (2007), *Journal of Personality Assessment*, 88(2), 187–204; parent scale Fraley, Waller & Brennan (2000) is public domain | Freely reproduced in the literature; non-commercial use with citation |
| Conflict communication | Communication Patterns Questionnaire — Short Form | Christensen & Heavey (1990), *JPSP*, 59(1); scoring per Crenshaw, Christensen, Baucom & Epstein (2017), *Psychological Assessment*, 29(7) | Unpublished academic instrument, freely circulated for research/clinical use |
| Commitment, investment, alternatives | Investment Model Scale (global items) | Rusbult, Martz & Agnew (1998), *Personal Relationships*, 5(4), 357–391 | Published in full in the journal appendix; distributed by Agnew's lab |
| Stay/leave ambivalence | Stay/leave reasons pool | Joel, MacDonald & Page-Gould (2018), *SPPS*, 9(6), 631–644 | Item content published with the paper |
| Abuse screening | Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST), gender-adapted | Brown, Lent, Brett, Sas & Pederson (1996), *Family Medicine*, 28(6), 422–428 | Widely reproduced in public-health practice with citation |
| Socially desirable responding | Marlowe–Crowne Form C | Reynolds (1982), *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, 38(1), 119–125 | Reproduced throughout the academic literature |
| Careless responding | Instructed-response items, response-time and long-string indices | Meade & Craig (2012), *Psychological Methods*, 17(3), 437–455; Ward & Meade (2023), *Annual Review of Psychology*, 74 | Method, not a licensed scale |

**Deliberately NOT used, with reasons:**
- **MBTI-style personality types** — poor test–retest stability of type assignments (Pittenger, 1993, *Review of Educational Research*, 63; 2005, *Consulting Psychology Journal*, 57; Stein & Swan, 2019, *Social & Personality Psychology Compass*).
- **Five Love Languages** — core claims unsupported (Impett, Park & Muise, 2024, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 33(2)); the quiz is proprietary.
- **Dyadic Adjustment Scale** — psychometrically superseded by the CSI (Funk & Rogge, 2007) and commercially licensed.
- **HITS abuse screen** — copyrighted with a licensing fee; WAST used instead.
- **"Divorce prediction percentage" of any kind** — see §4.

## 2. Interpretation thresholds (all published, none invented)

- CSI-16 total ranges 0–81; scores **below 51.5** indicate "notable relationship dissatisfaction" (Funk & Rogge, 2007). Aaina uses this cutoff and no other.
- ECR-S anxiety/avoidance are reported as **continuous dimensions** relative to the scale midpoint, never as attachment "types" — dimensional representation follows Fraley & Waller (1998, in *Attachment theory and close relationships*).
- CPQ demand/withdraw and constructive-communication composites follow the revised scoring of Crenshaw et al. (2017). Higher demand/withdraw is robustly associated with lower satisfaction (Christensen & Heavey, 1990; Eldridge & Christensen, 2002).
- Investment Model subscale means (0–8): commitment is the primary stay/leave signal, with satisfaction, alternatives, and investment as its published antecedents (Rusbult et al., 1998; meta-analytically confirmed by Le & Agnew, 2003, *Personal Relationships*, 10(1)).
- Ambivalence: simultaneous above-midpoint endorsement of stay AND leave reasons is the norm among people contemplating separation — roughly half of Joel et al.'s (2018) samples — so Aaina treats ambivalence as a finding to name, not an error to hide.
- WAST: any positive response on the two WAST-Short items, or an elevated pattern across the full tool, switches the report into the safety-first variant. Screening psychometrics are honest-labelled: no IPV screening tool has fully established properties (Rabin, Jennings, Campbell & Bair-Merritt, 2009, *American Journal of Preventive Medicine*, 36(5)).

## 3. What actually predicts staying vs leaving (the verdict engine's spine)

- **Le, Dove, Agnew, Korn & Mutso (2010)**, *Personal Relationships*, 17(3), 377–390 — meta-analysis of 137 studies (N=37,761): commitment, love, and dependence are the strongest dissolution predictors; satisfaction, alternatives, and investment moderate; social-network support robust.
- **Joel et al. (2020)**, *PNAS*, 117(32), 19061–19071 — machine learning across 43 longitudinal couple studies: the strongest self-report predictors of relationship quality are perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, and conflict. Critically: **one's own perceptions carry nearly all the predictive signal** — the scientific basis for Aaina's solo mode.
- **Karney & Bradbury (1995)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, 118(1), 3–34 — the vulnerability–stress–adaptation model: outcomes emerge from enduring vulnerabilities × external stress × adaptive processes. No single factor decides.

## 4. Honesty about limits (what Aaina refuses to claim)

1. **No prediction percentages.** Claims of "predicting divorce with 90%+ accuracy" are postdictive model-fitting; on cross-validation, accuracy collapsed (Heyman & Slep, 2001, *Journal of Marriage and Family*, 63(2), 473–479). Aaina describes present patterns and their published associations — it does not forecast a couple's future, because the evidence says change over time is largely unpredictable (Joel et al., 2020).
2. **No lie-proofness.** A motivated responder can defeat any self-report questionnaire. The literature supports detecting *careless* responding well (Meade & Craig, 2012) and detecting *deliberate faking* only weakly at the individual level. Aaina therefore (a) designs for honesty — privacy, no accounts, "no right answers" framing; (b) flags data-quality problems visibly in its confidence meter; (c) uses Marlowe–Crowne Form C only to *soften* interpretation, never to accuse (per critiques that social-desirability scales partly measure real traits: McCrae & Costa, 1983; Uziel, 2010).
3. **Solo ≠ couple equivalence.** Solo results rest on one person's perceptions. Those perceptions are the best-validated single signal we have (Joel et al., 2020), and the report says exactly this: a confident reading of the pattern as you experience it, honestly labelled as one-sided input.
4. **A screening is not a diagnosis, and a report is not therapy.** Aaina names patterns and cites evidence; it does not diagnose disorders, and it routes danger and acute distress to humans (see §6).

## 5. How the report speaks (delivery science)

The report's structure and tone implement published counselling frameworks, mapped to twenty
mechanical rules (R1–R20 in `research/lane2-counsellor-craft.md`):
Motivational Interviewing and reactance research (Miller & Rollnick; SAMHSA TIP 35) · the
SPIKES bad-news protocol (Baile et al., 2000, *The Oncologist*, 5(4)) · Therapeutic Assessment
feedback ordering (Finn; per Swann's self-verification theory) · self-affirmation before
threatening information (Sherman, 2013, *Social & Personality Psychology Compass*) ·
pattern-not-person framing from EFT (Johnson) and Gottman's process research · and the
three-paths structure of Discernment Counseling (Doherty, Harris & Wilde, 2016, *JMFT*, 42(2),
246–255), which is why Aaina never issues commands about your decision: it gives a confident
reading of the evidence and keeps the choice where it belongs — with you.

The anti-Barnum rule: every sentence in a report must be tied to the reader's own recorded
answers and must be capable of being false for other readers (Forer, 1949, on the fallacy
Aaina is built against). The renderer refuses uncited prose by construction.

## 6. Safety (sources in research/lane6-safety.md)

Disclosure-moment microcopy follows WHO's LIVES first-line support (WHO, 2013, *Responding to
intimate partner violence and sexual violence against women*). Abuse is never framed as a
mutual "cycle" — coercion is attributed to the person doing it (per IPV-informed practice
standards). Helplines listed are verified current as of 2026-08-24 (181, 112, Tele-MANAS
14416, iCall, Vandrevala, AASRA, Shakti Shalini, NCW, SIF-One); the discontinued KIRAN line is
excluded. Safety answers are processed in memory only and never persisted, exchanged, sent to
any server, printed, or shared.

## 7. Adaptations made, honestly logged

- WAST is administered gender-neutrally (original validation was with women; Aaina serves all
  users). This is an adaptation, labelled as such; screening thresholds unchanged.
- CPQ items are phrased gender-neutrally ("I"/"my partner" rather than husband/wife roles),
  consistent with contemporary research use of the instrument.
- Response formats preserved from the originals; where an item's idiom was Americanized, the
  Indian-English wording keeps the construct and is noted in the item bank comments.
- Jhalak (the 2-minute glimpse) reuses a subset of validated items but is explicitly labelled
  a glimpse, not a result; its output cites only what those items measure.
